## Abstract

Sparse mixture-of-experts (MoE) models route molecules to specialized subnetworks, but chemical differences between expert groups do not show that routing, or training the router, matters. We evaluated a sparse top-K MoE plug-in for graph convolutional networks (GCN) on ten MoleculeNet datasets and the 22-dataset absorption, distribution, metabolism, excretion and toxicity (ADMET) benchmark of the Therapeutics Data Commons (TDC), with scaffold splits (two ablation datasets excepted), and tested the router's contribution with routing and capacity controls. MoE-GCN had lower mean error than plain GCN in ten of twelve regression comparisons (nine of eleven counting Lipophilicity once); classification gains were inconsistent, and an ablation found no significant difference from two dense alternatives. Expert assignments were associated with physicochemical descriptors on four datasets (aromatic-ring count most consistently) but only modestly reproducible across seeds (adjusted Rand index 0.25–0.33). An untrained random gate differed little in test error from the learned router on four regression datasets (mean paired difference ≤ 0.007 MAE, exploratory). On Tox21 with a trainable gate (K ≥ 2), aromatic-ring η² was 0.31–0.37, at K = 2 a frozen random gate reached 0.29 against 0.37 for the learned gate (paired difference +0.09, 95% CI −0.02 to +0.19, five seeds) with the same validation AUROC. Substantial organization therefore emerges without optimizing the gate, although the trainable backbone may adapt to the fixed gate, and we detect no consistent accuracy benefit from training the router under the tested conditions. Across 21 datasets, endpoint-relevant descriptors were specialized more strongly (Spearman ρ = 0.43, 95% CI 0.21–0.59), but the largest descriptor effect was not detectably associated with MoE gain (ρ = −0.17, 95% CI −0.60 to 0.31).

**Scientific Contribution.** We provide controls separating the predictive contribution of sparse expert routing from parameter capacity and from the chemical organization of expert assignments. Substantial physicochemical descriptor association can emerge without optimizing the gate, and no consistent predictive benefit from gate training was detected, offering practical controls for testing claims of chemical specialization in molecular mixture-of-experts models.

**Keywords:** Molecular property prediction; Mixture of experts; Graph neural networks; ADMET; Interpretability; Sparse routing; Benchmarking

## Introduction

Unfavorable absorption, distribution, metabolism, excretion and toxicity (ADMET) properties remain a leading cause of late-stage attrition in drug development, where a single failure can represent a substantial fraction of the reported US$2.6 billion average cost of bringing a drug to market [1]. Reliable computational prediction of ADMET endpoints from molecular structure therefore allows unpromising candidates to be filtered before costly synthesis and assay, and has become a routine component of early-stage virtual screening, with public benchmarks such as the Therapeutics Data Commons (TDC) ADMET suite [2] and MoleculeNet [3] now serving as standard testbeds for such models.

Graph neural networks—graph convolutional networks [4], graph isomorphism networks [5], directed message-passing networks [6], graph-attention models [7], and large pretrained variants [8]—are the dominant approach for learning molecular properties directly from structure. These models share a structural assumption that is rarely examined: a single shared set of parameters maps every input molecule to its predicted property, regardless of where that molecule sits in chemical space. This forces, for instance, a small polar sugar and a large lipophilic steroid through the same weights, even though their solubility is plausibly governed by largely different structural features, which may dilute the signal available to each.

Sparse mixture-of-experts (MoE) architectures, originally developed to scale language models, address exactly this kind of heterogeneity by replacing a single computation path with a collection of expert subnetworks and a lightweight router that activates only a few experts per input [9–11]. Whether the router needs to be learned at all is itself an open question in that literature: fixed hash routing and frozen random routers have matched or outperformed learned routers in language-model experiments [23,24]. Applied to molecules, the appeal is intuitive: chemical space has natural cluster structure—drug-like, lipophilic, polar, fragment-like—and a router could in principle assign each cluster to a dedicated expert learning its specific structure–property mechanism. Prior molecular MoE work [12–15] has begun to explore this idea—via collaborative multi-task experts, BRICS-guided substructure routing, hybrid refinement layers, and multi-view fusion across string and graph encodings—but shares two limitations: chemical knowledge is either imposed on the router by construction rather than discovered [13], or expert behavior is reported qualitatively without a null hypothesis, a parameter-matched control, or cross-dataset replication [12,14,15]. The closest structural analogue is TopExpert [20], where experts likewise align with a molecular axis; there, however, the axis is topology and experts are trained via explicit clustering, whereas our experts arise from end-to-end sparse routing without explicit physicochemical or expert-assignment supervision, and align with physicochemical rather than topological structure.

This leaves two questions open. First, does end-to-end MoE routing discover chemically meaningful partitions, or merely a convenient load-balancing artefact? Second, are any predictive gains caused by specialized routing, or by the extra parameter capacity of the expert bank? A router that separates "lipophilic" from "polar" molecules on one dataset may be capturing a dataset-specific artefact, and a gain attributed to routing may simply reflect a larger effective model. Answering either question requires a null hypothesis, a controlled comparison against non-routed baselines, and replication across datasets; without them, the claim that "experts specialize chemically" cannot be distinguished from coincidence.

Here we treat both questions as hypotheses to be tested. We add a sparse top-K MoE module as an architecture-agnostic plug-in to standard graph neural network backbones and benchmark it on ten MoleculeNet datasets and the 22-dataset TDC ADMET suite, using scaffold splits except where stated (Methods). We then ask what the router itself contributes, using controls that share the backbone, hyper-parameters, split and training protocol (a frozen random gate, fixed k-means and random assignments, and forced expert swaps) alongside dense baselines. We report accuracy as it is, including where the plug-in does not help. The answer is narrower than the premise suggests: expert assignments are associated with physicochemical descriptors, but much of that structure is present without a trained router, and we detect no consistent accuracy benefit from training it.

## Results

### Sparse routing provides a testable expectation of chemically specialized computation

Sparse mixture-of-experts architectures assume that different molecules benefit from different computational pathways, but whether such specialization actually emerges during molecular property prediction cannot be inferred from the architecture alone. The model treats prediction as a routing problem (Fig. 1). A standard graph neural network compresses a molecule into a single representation vector; that vector is passed to a small bank of expert subnetworks together with a learned router, and the router forwards each molecule to the few experts best suited to it. Only those experts compute, and their outputs are combined into the final prediction.

Crucially, the router receives no explicit physicochemical descriptors, scaffold labels, or expert-assignment supervision; routing is learned end-to-end from the molecular representation and the prediction objective alone, with a mild pressure, through a load-balancing term, not to ignore any expert. Any chemical structure in the resulting assignment must therefore arise without direct chemical supervision, through end-to-end optimization of the backbone and, for K ≥ 2, the router (for K = 1 the router receives no gradient; see Methods). If experts acquire chemically distinct roles during training, the resulting partition should be detectable independently of predictive performance—the basis on which we examine routing below, before turning to benchmark accuracy.

Because the module attaches to the pooled representation and leaves the underlying network untouched, the same component can be dropped into different backbones. We use a graph convolutional network as the primary backbone and report its behavior throughout, returning to the other backbones only to test transferability.

### Regression gains are modest and not consistent across tasks

If sparse routing captures meaningful heterogeneity within chemical space, its benefits should appear preferentially on tasks whose underlying structure–property relationships are themselves heterogeneous; regression benchmarks, where chemically distinct molecular classes often contribute through different mechanisms to the same continuous endpoint, provide a direct test of that expectation. On regression tasks, adding the expert plug-in to the graph convolutional backbone improved accuracy on most of the tested regression datasets (Table 1). Across the three MoleculeNet regression sets and nine TDC regression sets, the MoE model reduced error on ten of twelve datasets, with the largest gains on the chemically diverse solubility and solvation benchmarks: a 4.7% reduction in root-mean-square error on ESOL (1.067 versus 1.120 ± 0.036, paired one-sided *t*-test *P* = 0.017), 1.7% on FreeSolv (3.591 versus 3.654 ± 0.544; not significant at the per-dataset level, paired one-sided *t*-test *P* = 0.31, though MoE-GCN's lower mean still counts as a win for the pooled sign test below, which evaluates win/loss direction rather than per-dataset significance), a further 3.1% on Lipophilicity (0.722 versus 0.745 ± 0.007, paired one-sided *t*-test *P* < 0.001), and 20.8% in mean absolute error on Caco-2 permeability (0.365 versus 0.461; per-dataset-tuned three-seed values, Table 2 — distinct from the fixed-architecture dense-baseline ablation reported in Table 3). GCN is reported as mean ± s.d. over the same five matched scaffold-split seeds and hyperparameter search budget as MoE-GCN on all three MoleculeNet regression datasets (see Methods). Pooled at the dataset level across all twelve regression datasets, MoE-GCN won on ten and lost on two, which by an exact one-sided sign test gives *P* = 0.019 if the twelve comparisons are treated as independent. That assumption is questionable: Lipophilicity appears in both suites, and counting it once gives 9 wins of 11 (*P* = 0.033); further excluding the two datasets on which routing collapsed to a single expert (Clearance-microsome and PPBR, where there is effectively no routing) gives 7 of 9 (*P* = 0.090; Table 4); a Wilcoxon signed-rank test on the same signed differences gives a consistent result (*P* = 0.017, rank-biserial correlation 0.69) and is reported as a sensitivity analysis, since it ranks the magnitude of RMSE, MAE, and Spearman differences together despite their different scales, whereas the sign test avoids that issue by testing only win/loss counts (Table 4). Both losses occurred on the two smallest regression datasets (half-life and hepatocyte clearance, each under 500 training molecules), consistent with, but insufficient to establish, a small-data limitation (see Limitations).

The regression trend was not mirrored in classification, where MoE-GCN showed no consistent benefit. On the five MoleculeNet classification datasets where GCN was rerun under the matched protocol (ToxCast and HIV were not), MoE-GCN had a higher mean AUROC on two (BBBP, +0.112; Tox21, +0.011) and a lower one on three (BACE, −0.071; ClinTox, −0.022; SIDER, −0.009). The mean difference was +0.004 AUROC (95% CI −0.080 to +0.088, *n* = 5; exact one-sided sign test *P* = 0.81, Wilcoxon *P* = 0.59) and is driven by BBBP; BACE is the clearest case of the plug-in underperforming (matched GCN 0.964 ± 0.014 against 0.893 ± 0.051). These five comparisons are exploratory; per-dataset TDC classification results are in Table 2. We do not report a single pooled statistic across all 22 TDC datasets, since that would mix classification and regression metrics on the same incommensurate scales the sign test was designed to avoid for the regression-only pool; the per-dataset breakdown in Table 2 is the appropriate way to read the full TDC picture. We report these null results directly rather than selectively: in our benchmarks, the observed gains were concentrated in regression tasks, whereas classification showed no consistent benefit.

Two further comparisons bound the practical value of the gain. Against a stronger attention-based backbone (AttentiveFP), the expert model had a higher mean RMSE on all three MoleculeNet regression datasets, with large margins on FreeSolv (3.59 versus 2.57) and Lipophilicity (0.722 versus 0.685) but a difference within one standard deviation on ESOL (1.067 versus 1.061; Table 1); attention applied during message passing and routing applied after pooling address complementary parts of the architecture, and our post-pooling plug-in does not subsume the former. Against a model pretrained on ten million molecules (GROVER), the gap was larger still on several tasks, though GROVER's values are taken from its original random-split protocol and are not independently reproduced under our scaffold-split protocol (Table 1, †).

EC-MPNN — an edge-conditioned message-passing network (NNConv with a GRUCell update); we verified against the source that it does not implement the reverse-bond message exclusion that defines Yang et al.'s original D-MPNN, so we do not claim D-MPNN fidelity — showed the same directional benefit as GCN on two of three datasets: MoE routing improved RMSE on ESOL (1.105 versus 1.163) and FreeSolv (2.865 versus 3.089) but not on Lipophilicity (0.712 versus 0.701). This suggests the plug-in's benefit, while not universal, transfers more consistently across backbones than a single-dataset failure would imply, an open question we return to in the Discussion. Cross-architecture transferability to GIN has not yet been tested and we make no claim about it here.

### No significant routing-specific accuracy difference was detected against dense baselines

Because a larger model can outperform a smaller one for reasons unrelated to routing, we compared MoE-GCN against two dense routing-free baselines and a plain-GCN reference (Table 3; Dense-uniform is within 0.3% of MoE-GCN's parameter count, Dense-wide is 65% larger): Dense-uniform, in which the same expert subnetworks are retained but averaged with equal, non-learned weights, and Dense-wide, a single wider feed-forward layer. Across five regression datasets and five seeds each, MoE-GCN showed no significant difference from Dense-uniform or Dense-wide in dataset-level tests on relative RMSE differences, (other − MoE-GCN) / MoE-GCN (Dense-uniform: paired *t*-test *P* = 0.71, Wilcoxon *P* = 1.0; Dense-wide: *t*-test *P* = 0.79, Wilcoxon *P* = 0.81; *n* = 5 datasets, two-sided; with five datasets the smallest attainable Wilcoxon *P* is 0.0625, so these tests are descriptive); no configuration dominated across all five datasets. A plain GCN backbone — no expert layer, no dense-ensemble layer, just the backbone and task head — was added to this comparison under the identical fixed-architecture protocol for all five datasets. Plain-GCN did not merely match the other three configurations: it had the lowest RMSE outright on two of five datasets, ESOL (1.102, versus 1.108 for the next best, Dense-wide) and Lipophilicity (0.752, versus 0.764 for the next best, Dense-uniform), was competitive on FreeSolv (3.119, within range of the other three configurations' 3.04–3.09), and trailed the other three by a wider margin on Caco-2 and AqSolDB-Solubility, the two datasets on which Plain-GCN's random split cannot be confirmed to match the other arms (Methods), so we do not interpret those two differences. Because the Plain-GCN comparison is interpretable only on the three scaffold-split datasets, we do not test it formally (with three datasets no Wilcoxon test can reach *P* < 0.25) and report intervals instead (Table 3). No single architectural addition among routing, dense ensembling, or extra width shows a reliable improvement over an unmodified backbone on the three datasets where that comparison is interpretable, although so few datasets give the comparison low power and a non-significant difference is not evidence of equivalence. In relative terms, (other − MoE-GCN) / MoE-GCN averaged over the five datasets was −0.3% (95% CI −2.0 to +1.5) for Dense-uniform, −0.3% (−3.3 to +2.7) for Dense-wide (*t*-based intervals over five datasets; negative means the other model had lower error). For Plain-GCN, restricted to ESOL, FreeSolv and Lipophilicity, the mean was −1.2% (95% CI −8.2 to +5.8; *t*-based with two degrees of freedom, so very wide and sensitive to the method, *n* = 3; per dataset −1.4%, +1.7% and −3.9%), so Plain-GCN was nominally better than MoE-GCN on two of three. Differences of a few percent in either direction cannot be excluded.

No significant difference between sparse routing and the dense alternatives was detected. The distinction is not that only routing produces chemically organized structure — the frozen-gate control below shows that substantial descriptor organization emerges even without optimizing the gate, although the trainable backbone may have adapted to it — but that routing provides an endogenous, prediction-coupled assignment: each molecule's expert label is generated by the model itself as part of computing its prediction, whereas a dense baseline has no analogous internal mechanism from which a comparable per-molecule label could be read off without a separate, post-hoc step.

### Specialization depends on K, but a random gate keeps most of it

We trained the same MoE-GCN with K = 1, 2 and 4 on Tox21 (classification, four experts) and AstraZeneca Lipophilicity (regression, four experts), three seeds each (five for K = 2 on Tox21), logging expert load, gate-weight norm and validation score every epoch and recomputing η² at the best validation epoch (Fig. 2, Table 5; `exp2_topk_dynamics.py`). With four experts, K = 4 activates every expert with softmax weights, so it is a dense-gating control and not sparse selection; only K = 1 and K = 2 are sparse. On Tox21, K = 1 produced weak, seed-dependent specialization (η² for aromatic rings 0.078 ± 0.044), whereas K = 2 and K = 4 produced much stronger and more consistent effects (0.372 ± 0.072 with five seeds and 0.312 ± 0.068 with three; LogP 0.140 ± 0.050 and 0.173 ± 0.095). Validation AUROC did not improve with K: it was highest at K = 1 (0.771 ± 0.004) and about 0.01 lower at K = 2 and 4 (0.762 ± 0.003 and 0.759 ± 0.001), a difference larger than the seed spread. These are validation scores, not test scores. The Tox21 load curves (Fig. 2, seed 0) differ clearly by K. At K = 1 the load swung between near-zero and about 0.95 on a single expert during roughly the first 20 epochs, then settled with one expert holding about 0.58 of molecules and the others 0.09–0.18; this is the pattern earlier drafts described as an exploration-to-specialization transition. At K = 2 and K = 4 no abrupt transition occurred: all four experts stayed between about 0.2 and 0.35 of the load throughout, with fluctuations damping after about 25 epochs. The earlier transition therefore describes the K = 1 router, whose gate-weight norm had already collapsed to near zero by epoch 3, and not router training in general. The Lipophilicity load curves (Fig. 2, seed 0) show the same K = 1 pattern. Loads swung between near 0 and near 1 on single experts for about the first 38 epochs, then settled abruptly, with one expert holding about 0.65 of molecules, two holding about 0.2 and 0.14 and the fourth near zero; the gate-weight norm had already fallen to zero within about three epochs in every seed, so this settling is not driven by gate learning (we did not test what does drive it). At K = 2 the load oscillated for about 30 epochs and then damped, but only two of four experts ever held a noticeable share of molecules (seed 0: about 0.55 and 0.45, the other two at zero from the start), and across seeds only 2.3 ± 0.6 of four experts were active at the best epoch. At K = 4 all four experts were active and the loads damped gradually (about 0.37, 0.31, 0.18 and 0.14 at the end for seed 0), with the fourth expert starting to take load only after about 20 epochs. Gate-weight norms rose from about 1.2 to 1.4–2.0 at K = 2 and K = 4. That only about two experts carried load at K = 2 is a further reason that η² is small on this dataset, since few groups limit the descriptor contrast.

The gate behaved as the implementation predicts. At K = 1 the gate-weight norm fell from about 1.2 at initialization to 0.25–0.29 after the first epoch and to 0.000 by the end, in every seed of both datasets, whereas at K ≥ 2 it grew (Tox21: 2.14 ± 0.23 and 2.21 ± 0.19 at K = 2 and 4). A plausible mechanism is that Adam (the released code uses `torch.optim.Adam` with coupled L2 weight decay) rescales the small weight-decay gradient, which is the only gradient the K = 1 gate receives, so each weight moves about one learning-rate step per update; we did not test this directly.

To ask whether the stronger specialization at K ≥ 2 requires a trained router, we repeated K = 2 on Tox21 with the gate frozen at its random initialization (`--frozen_gate`), for five paired seeds (same seed, split and initialization as the learned arm). Training the gate did not change validation AUROC (learned 0.762 ± 0.003, frozen 0.762 ± 0.004; paired difference −0.001, 95% CI −0.009 to +0.008, *P* = 0.81). Descriptor effects were higher with the learned gate on average but not significantly so: aromatic rings 0.372 ± 0.072 versus 0.286 ± 0.110 (paired difference +0.086, 95% CI −0.015 to +0.186, *P* = 0.078; higher in 4 of 5 seeds) and LogP 0.140 ± 0.050 versus 0.095 ± 0.042 (+0.045, −0.002 to +0.093, *P* = 0.058; higher in 5 of 5 seeds but by 0.004 in one). The six remaining descriptors differed by at most 0.04 with intervals spanning zero (uncorrected *P* ≥ 0.28). A frozen random gate therefore reproduced about three quarters of the aromatic-ring effect, so substantial descriptor association emerges without optimizing the gate (the trainable backbone may have adapted to the fixed gate), and training the gate adds at most a modest, inconsistent increment that five seeds cannot resolve. Cross-seed partition agreement was low for both arms (mean pairwise adjusted Rand index 0.26 learned, 0.20 frozen; no test was run on this difference). Freezing the gate does not freeze the backbone, which can adapt to the fixed assignment; the contrast therefore isolates the value of training the gate parameters, not of routing-dependent learning as a whole. On Lipophilicity, specialization remained small at every K (aromatic rings 0.051 ± 0.034, 0.033 ± 0.003 and 0.038 ± 0.014 for K = 1, 2, 4), so the weak effect on that dataset is not explained by the K = 1 router.

### Expert assignments are associated with physicochemical descriptors, weakly on one dataset

If different experts encode different regions of chemical space, molecules assigned to separate experts should differ systematically in physicochemical properties not explicitly provided to the router during optimization; failure to observe such differences would indicate arbitrary optimization dynamics rather than genuine physicochemical structure. We quantified the relationship between dominant expert assignment and eight physicochemical descriptors across four ADMET datasets, recomputing the effect size separately for each of five training seeds and reporting mean ± s.d. (Fig. 3A; Table 6). On the three datasets other than AstraZeneca Lipophilicity, every descriptor differed significantly across experts after multiple-comparison correction in every seed. On aqueous solubility (*n* = 9,980; five to six active experts per seed) lipophilicity was the strongest axis (LogP η² = 0.27 ± 0.04), followed by aromatic-ring count (η² = 0.21 ± 0.07, range 0.16–0.34). Table 7 and Fig. 3B show the partition for one named seed (seed 0): one expert holds lipophilic, aromatic molecules (mean LogP 4.5, 1.7 aromatic rings), another hydrophilic, highly polar ones (mean LogP −0.3, polar surface area 95), and the remaining three differ mainly in size, aromaticity and polarity. A different seed gives a different set of groups (cross-seed ARI 0.23–0.33).

On Caco-2 permeability (*n* = 910) aromatic-ring count was the largest effect (η² = 0.32 ± 0.09), with polar surface area (0.19 ± 0.09), H-bond acceptors (0.18 ± 0.07) and LogP (0.16 ± 0.10) behind it; the LogP effect was seed-dependent (per-seed range 0.06–0.29). On acute toxicity (LD50, *n* = 7,385) aromatic-ring count was again the largest and most stable effect (η² = 0.39 ± 0.04), followed by ring count (0.28 ± 0.05); aromatic-ring count and ring count are correlated descriptors, so these should not be read as independent axes. Aromatic-ring count was therefore the largest single effect on three of three datasets with strong specialization, whereas LogP led only on solubility. Averaged over the four datasets, aromatic-ring count had mean η² = 0.24 against 0.15 for LogP (Table 6). Across the 21 TDC ADMET datasets with a non-collapsed router, aromatic-ring count had the largest η² of the eight descriptors on 17 (mean η² 0.25, against 0.13 for LogP and 0.16 for ring count; Table S3). Whether such axes reflect something the router learned or structure already present in the pooled representation is addressed by the controls below; LogP is one of the four Lipinski parameters [16], while aromatic-ring count is a related heuristic not part of that rule.

On the fourth dataset, AstraZeneca Lipophilicity (*n* = 4,200), specialization was small: no descriptor's mean η² exceeded 0.071 (MW 0.071 ± 0.027), and LogP (endpoint correlation 0.415, Table S3) was 0.022 ± 0.025. This dataset uses K = 1, for which the router receives no gradient; only two to four of the four experts were active in any seed, and the partition was not reproducible across seeds (below). We therefore tested the same model with K = 1, 2 and 4 (three seeds each; for K ≥ 2 the gate receives gradient and its weight norm grew from about 1.2 to 1.4–2.0, whereas at K = 1 it fell to 0.000 within the run). Specialization stayed small at every K (mean η² for LogP 0.015 ± 0.007, 0.013 ± 0.017 and 0.028 ± 0.013 for K = 1, 2, 4; for aromatic rings 0.051 ± 0.034, 0.033 ± 0.003 and 0.038 ± 0.014; no single-seed value above 0.09), and best validation MAE was similar (0.538 ± 0.007, 0.542 ± 0.015, 0.540 ± 0.019, with seed-to-seed spread of the same size as the differences between K). The K = 1 router therefore does not explain the weak specialization on this dataset, and training the router (K ≥ 2) did not increase it (`exp2_topk_dynamics.py`). The source literature describes this dataset as lower in chemical diversity; we measured two diversity metrics (Table S2) and they disagree. By mean pairwise ECFP4 Tanimoto distance it is marginally the least diverse of the four (0.881, versus 0.892–0.922 for the others), but by Bemis–Murcko scaffold-to-molecule ratio it is the *most* diverse (0.582, versus 0.206–0.536). We do not consider the low-diversity account established.

We tested a second candidate mechanism: that a descriptor strongly correlated with the prediction endpoint becomes task-relevant signal the network must preserve, rather than a nuisance axis free for the router to partition on, which predicts a negative relationship between |correlation(endpoint, descriptor)| and that descriptor's η². Across the 21 TDC ADMET datasets with a non-collapsed router (168 dataset–descriptor pairs, η² averaged over five seeds; Table S3), we found the opposite: |correlation| and η² were positively associated (pooled Spearman ρ = 0.43; 95% CI 0.21–0.59 from a bootstrap that resamples whole datasets, because pairs are nested within datasets). Within datasets, ρ was positive in 16 of 21 (median 0.33; zero in one) and negative in four: bioavailability, Caco-2, CYP2D6 substrate and AstraZeneca Lipophilicity; each within-dataset ρ rests on eight descriptors, so these values are coarse. Excluding the three datasets with fewer than three active experts in any seed gave ρ = 0.38 (95% CI 0.16–0.57), and dropping aromatic-ring count, ring count or both gave 0.45–0.50. A positive association does not show that routing uses endpoint-relevant descriptors: any partition of a representation that encodes the endpoint would be expected to separate descriptors that predict it, and this test cannot distinguish learned from untrained routing. On AstraZeneca Lipophilicity, LogP's endpoint correlation is high (0.415) but its η² is the lowest of the four datasets analysed across seeds (0.022 ± 0.025), so this dataset runs against the pooled trend. Suppression on this dataset is explained neither by chemical diversity (Table S2) nor by endpoint–descriptor correlation (Table S3), and the K = 2 and K = 4 runs above show that the K = 1 router is not the cause.


### Expert partitions are only modestly reproducible across seeds

Expert labels are arbitrary across training runs, so we compared partitions of the same molecules across the five seeds with the adjusted Rand index (ARI, permutation-invariant) and a permutation null. Pairwise ARI was modest on the datasets with the strongest specialization (mean 0.33 for AqSolDB, 0.25 for Caco-2 and 0.31 for LD50; pairwise minima 0.16–0.23; permutation null ≈ 0) — well above chance but far from identical partitions — and near zero for AstraZeneca Lipophilicity (mean 0.012, minimum −0.050). On that K = 1 dataset the largest expert held between 50% and 93% of molecules depending on the seed. Because the descriptor effects of Table 6 are similar across seeds although the partitions are not, the descriptors capture what the experts tend to encode, not a specific set of molecule groups.

### No consistent accuracy benefit from training the router was detected relative to a random fixed gate

The associations above leave open whether learning the router matters. We trained four arms with the same backbone, expert architecture, hyper-parameters, split and training protocol on the four regression datasets (Fig. 5, Table 8). The learned and frozen-gate arms use each dataset's tuned K, whereas the k-means and random arms are hard top-1, so only the learned-versus-frozen contrast is matched in K and in routing sparsity; the comparisons with the k-means and random arms also change the assignment rule and the sparsity, and are secondary. The arms are: the learned router; a *frozen random gate* (the gate is initialized randomly and never updated); *fixed k-means* routing (hard top-1 assignment from k-means on eight standardized RDKit descriptors, fitted on training molecules only); and *fixed random assignment* (a fixed random expert per molecule, unrelated to chemistry). Arms were compared with paired differences over seeds (five seeds; three for LD50).

*Random assignment was worse.* Compared with the learned router, fixed random assignment had higher test MAE on all four datasets (paired Δ in Table 8; *P* = 0.008 Caco-2, 0.007 Lipophilicity, 0.030 LD50 with three seeds, 0.13 Solubility). Because the fixed random arm also differs from the learned arm in K (hard top-1 versus the tuned K) and in expert utilization, this shows that the tested random partition performed worse, not that molecule dependence in itself is what helps; a K-matched random-routing control was not run.

*No consistent predictive benefit from training the router was detected.* A random gate that was never trained differed from the learned router by at most 0.007 MAE on every dataset (paired *P* between 0.61 and 0.89 on Caco-2, Solubility and Lipophilicity; on LD50, Δ = −0.001 with near-zero variance across three seeds, so its nominal *P* = 0.027 (uncorrected, three seeds) marks a statistically detectable but practically negligible difference of 0.0007 MAE). Fixed k-means routing on eight descriptors also showed no consistent difference: learned routing had a lower mean MAE on three datasets and a higher one on Solubility, none significant (*P* = 0.06–0.39; Lipophilicity borderline at *P* = 0.059 with learned lower in 4 of 5 seeds). Differences between arms were small relative to seed variability (s.d. 0.01–0.05 MAE). Paired 95% CIs (*t*-based, learned − frozen) were −0.002 (−0.046 to +0.041) on Caco-2, +0.005 (−0.055 to +0.065) on Solubility, −0.0007 (−0.0012 to −0.0002) on LD50 (three seeds; far below the seed s.d. of 0.014) and +0.007 (−0.027 to +0.040) on Lipophilicity. On Lipophilicity (K = 1) the learned gate receives no task gradient, so learned versus frozen is not an informative contrast there. All comparisons in this section are exploratory.

*Forced-swap tests show weak functional specialization.* For each trained model we forced every test molecule through every expert and compared the error of molecules routed naturally to a group with the error when forced to the other active experts (each forced prediction uses a single expert, whereas natural predictions at K ≥ 2 combine K experts; restricted to experts that receive ≥ 10 test molecules; dead experts otherwise inflate the ratio). The ratio of other-expert to own-expert error was 1.17 ± 0.12 (Caco-2), 1.38 ± 0.16 (Solubility), 1.25 ± 0.05 (LD50) and 0.99 ± 0.01 (Lipophilicity) for the learned router, against 1.13 ± 0.23, 1.19 ± 0.08, 1.31 ± 0.06 and 1.09 ± 0.05 for the frozen random gate. Swapping a molecule to another expert thus raises error by at most about 40% on average, and a router that was never trained produces comparable ratios; Solubility is the only dataset where the learned router is higher, and with five seeds we do not test that difference formally. Utilization was low: counting test molecules only (Table 6 counts all molecules), in the learned models only 3–7 of 16 experts (Caco-2), 4–6 of 8 (Solubility), 5–6 of 16 (LD50) and 2–4 of 4 (Lipophilicity) received ≥ 10 test molecules. On Lipophilicity (K = 1) the ratio was 0.99, so the active experts are interchangeable in function.

Together, these controls show that the tested fixed-random partition performed worse than learned routing (without isolating molecule dependence, since it also differs in K and utilization), but they give no evidence that training the router is responsible: an untrained gate and a simple descriptor k-means partition do as well in accuracy, and forced-swap functional specialization is weak in all arms.

## Discussion

Three findings separate the predictive and interpretive parts of this study, and all are qualified. Predictively, MoE-GCN has a lower mean error than plain GCN on ten of twelve regression comparisons, but classification gains are inconsistent and the five-dataset ablation detects no significant difference from dense alternatives; a non-significant difference is not evidence of equivalence. The controlled ablation, in which all arms share one backbone, one set of fixed hyper-parameters and one seed set, carries the stronger conclusion; the broader benchmark is treated as a directional trend because it rests on twelve dataset-level comparisons with margins of a few percent, on three seeds for TDC, and on a hyper-parameter search that is matched in trials and search space but not in dimensionality. Interpretively, expert assignment is associated with aromatic-ring count most consistently and with lipophilicity on solubility, weakly on a fourth dataset, and the partitions are only partly reproducible across seeds. Mechanistically, routing controls that share the backbone, split and training protocol (but not K for the k-means and random arms) show that random assignment is worse, whereas no consistent difference in accuracy was detected between the learned router and an untrained random gate or a fixed descriptor k-means partition; on Tox21, a frozen gate retains most of the specialization that a trained gate (K ≥ 2) produces, and forced-swap tests show weak functional specialization. We discuss each below.

### Predictive performance of sparse MoE molecular plug-ins

Sparse mixture-of-experts models are often motivated by the widely hypothesized advantage that heterogeneous chemical space should be represented by heterogeneous computation, but until now the evidence supporting this advantage has remained largely qualitative. Our benchmarking shows a directional, bounded regression advantage that is not isolated to routing: MoE has lower error on ten of twelve datasets, significant when pooled at the dataset level only under an independence assumption (exact sign test *P* = 0.019; 0.033 with Lipophilicity counted once; 0.090 also excluding collapsed-routing datasets), but is outperformed by an attention-based backbone on regression, does not reach the accuracy reported for a model pretrained on ten million molecules on most tasks (though those values come from a different, random-split protocol and are not directly comparable to our scaffold-split results — see Table 1), and—critically—by two dense baselines (Dense-uniform, matched in parameter count; Dense-wide, 65% larger) with no significant difference in either direction. This does not establish that routing is responsible for the improvement over the smaller plain GCN: dense models of comparable capacity were not significantly different from MoE-GCN in a five-dataset comparison with limited power, so additional capacity remains a plausible explanation for the gain over the plain backbone. This qualified picture contrasts with Viganò et al. [21], who report MoE outperforming its baselines in a cardiotoxicity multitask setting. Their gain arises from a multitask architecture that shares experts across related endpoints, whereas our ablation isolates routing within a single fixed representation and a single task per model — a materially different comparison, not a contradiction. We do not compare against the Chemprop or TDC leaderboards directly, since our comparison of interest is an ablation within a fixed backbone rather than a cross-method performance ranking.

### Descriptor separation does not require a trained router

We used three controls to ask what the router contributes to the chemical organization of experts. First, a frozen random gate at K = 2 on Tox21 reproduced about 70–77% of the learned router's η² for LogP and aromatic rings with indistinguishable validation AUROC; the learned router was higher on average, but the paired differences were not significant with five seeds (Table 5). Second, on four regression datasets a frozen gate, the other K-matched contrast, showed no consistent test-error difference from the learned router (Table 8); fixed k-means routing on eight descriptors (hard top-1, so not K-matched) performed comparably, and is a secondary architectural control rather than a test of router learning. Third, forced-swap tests found only weak functional specialization in either arm (error ratios 1.0–1.4, Table 8). Fixed random assignment (also hard top-1) was worse than learned routing, but because it differs in K and utilization as well, we do not read that as isolating molecule dependence. The primary evidence on router training is the learned-versus-frozen contrast, which detected no consistent accuracy benefit from training the router under the tested conditions.

We also asked whether stronger specialization is associated with larger predictive benefit. For the 21 TDC ADMET datasets with a non-collapsed router we took the largest single-descriptor η² (mean over five seeds, Table S3) and correlated it with MoE-GCN's percentage gain over plain GCN (RMSE or MAE reduction, or Spearman/AUROC increase, as reported per dataset; the gains mix metrics and are therefore only a rough common scale). Spearman ρ was −0.17 (95% CI −0.60 to 0.31, bootstrap over datasets; *P* = 0.48), and using the mean η² over the eight descriptors gave −0.15 (−0.61 to 0.33). The interval is wide, so a moderate association in either direction cannot be excluded with 21 datasets, but there is no sign that datasets with stronger descriptor association gain more. An earlier version of this analysis used a composite score discounted by routing entropy; because load balancing pushes entropy up and uniform expert usage is not weak specialization, we replaced it with the descriptor effect alone.

These results are compatible with an account in which the backbone representation already encodes physicochemical structure and any gate, trained or not, partitions it along those directions; router training may sharpen or stabilize the partition, but the present data (five seeds, one classification dataset for the K ≥ 2 contrast) cannot show that. The routing partition does have a practical property that a post-hoc analysis lacks: it is produced inside the model and can be read out without a separate step. This parallels language-model MoE reports in which hash-based or frozen random routers matched learned ones [23,24]; what we add is a test of that question for molecular property prediction, together with a descriptor-level analysis of how expert assignments are organized.

Aromatic-ring count and ring count are also the descriptors most directly available to the model from its inputs, because the nine atom features include aromaticity and ring membership; their prominence among expert axes (aromatic rings had the largest η² on 17 of 21 datasets) may therefore reflect what the backbone represents most easily, not what a router selected, and this is consistent with the frozen-gate result.

This addresses a gap in prior molecular MoE work, which either builds chemical knowledge into the router by construction [13] or reports expert behavior qualitatively without a null hypothesis, a frozen-router control, or replication [12,14,15]. Our controls show that descriptor association alone is not evidence that a router learned chemistry.

### Limitations of the present study

Several limitations bound these conclusions. First, the plug-in does not deliver state-of-the-art accuracy, reflecting where it operates—after pooling rather than inside message passing—and that it trains from scratch rather than leveraging pretraining; inserting the same routing into an attention-based or pretrained backbone is a natural extension. Second, the accuracy benefit, where present, is concentrated in regression, and the classification comparisons are inconsistent (two of five matched MoleculeNet comparisons favor MoE-GCN); both observed regression losses occurred on the two smallest datasets in our regression set (half-life and hepatocyte clearance, each under 500 training molecules), which is suggestive but not sufficient evidence to establish a general small-data boundary from only two instances, and we observed one expert collapsing to a few percent of the load on a small classification set. Third, cross-architecture transferability is itself architecture-dependent: EC-MPNN gained on ESOL and FreeSolv but lost on Lipophilicity, a partial rather than consistent match to the gains observed with GCN. GIN was excluded from all interpretability analysis in this study and its transferability remains untested. GIN's strictly greater graph-isomorphism discriminative power may alter routing partitioning patterns relative to GCN, so this exclusion should not be read as an implicit negative result; we make no claim about GIN either way. Fourth, and most importantly, the dense-baseline ablation does not establish that the improvement over plain GCN is attributable to routing — dense models of comparable or larger size were not significantly different, in a comparison with limited power — but our evidence that routing is chemically meaningful remains correlational: expert assignment tracks physicochemical descriptors, but we have not isolated the causal effect of changing a molecule's natural routing decision while preserving the model's top-K aggregation. A forced-swap test (molecules explicitly assigned to single alternative experts, which replaces the natural top-K combination with one expert) and learned-versus-frozen-gate and fixed-routing controls are reported (Fig. 5, Table 8), but only on four regression datasets with one backbone. Three concrete follow-ups would strengthen the causal case: (i) the same controls with K ≥ 2 on the K = 1 datasets, on classification, and on other backbones, with shifts in latent representations quantified; (ii) router feature occlusion, zeroing physicochemically relevant latent dimensions to test whether aromatic-ring or LogP partitioning collapses; and (iii) controlled synthetic compound libraries, curating matched molecule pairs differing only in aromatic-ring count or LogP to isolate single physicochemical axes. None of (i)–(iii) was performed here, and we flag them explicitly as the most important remaining work rather than as completed analysis. Finally, our replication spans four datasets chosen to span a range of chemical diversity by design, though our direct measurement of that diversity (Table S2) did not cleanly separate the fourth dataset from the other three, and a second candidate mechanism — suppression of specialization on descriptors correlated with the endpoint — was not supported at the pooled level and does not explain this dataset's anomaly either (Table S3); extending this to more datasets with independently characterized diversity, and to a direct causal test of what this dataset's representation is doing differently, would better map the boundary conditions under which the recovered axes emerge. Fifth, in the released implementation the router is untrained under K = 1 (the softmax over a single selected logit is identically one; verified, gate gradient 0 at K = 1), so earlier K = 1 analyses should not be read as router behavior. We reran Tox21 and Lipophilicity at K = 1, 2 and 4 (Table 5), but the frozen-gate contrast at K ≥ 2 covers one classification dataset, one K and five seeds, and the learned-versus-frozen differences in η² were not significant at that size (intervals include zero, so a modest effect cannot be excluded). Sixth, the routing controls (Fig. 5, Table 8) have limits: they cover four regression datasets with one backbone (GCN), three to five seeds (three for LD50), so small differences cannot be excluded; the fixed k-means and random arms use hard top-1 routing whereas the learned and frozen arms use each dataset's tuned K (4, 3, 2 and 1), so the arms differ in more than the source of the assignment; and expert partitions are only modestly reproducible across seeds (ARI 0.25–0.33). The forced-swap measure and the controls were designed before the results were seen, but the forced-swap ratio was initially computed over all experts, which inflated it through unused experts; the active-expert values reported here are the corrected ones. Classification datasets were not part of the controls. Seventh, the router sees only the pooled representation of a backbone whose atom features include aromaticity and ring membership, so aromatic-ring specialization is not evidence of chemical discovery in the strong sense. The k-means control on pooled representations (formerly Fig. 3C) was removed because its Methods and Results descriptions could not be reconciled; the routing controls of Table 8 and Table 5 replace it.

### Recommendations for future molecular MoE benchmarking

On crowded molecular benchmarks, MoE plug-ins yield modest and uneven accuracy gains that are easily matched by stronger backbones, pretraining, or a closely parameter-matched dense ensemble, and a contribution resting on leaderboard position is fragile. The more durable contribution is the structure of the learned routing itself, but establishing that durability requires two distinct kinds of evidence, not one: whether the plug-in's predictive gain is routing-specific is a question for a parameter-matched dense control, while whether the resulting chemical organization is routing-specific is a separate question for a clustering or random-partition null — a dense model has no routing assignment to test in the first place, so it cannot serve as the control for the interpretability claim. Both controls should be run and reported, and neither substitutes for the other. We recommend four concrete directions: integrating MoE routing inside the graph neural network message-passing layers rather than only after pooling; combining the plug-in with self-supervised molecular pretraining in the style of GROVER; testing routing generalization on external, proprietary compound libraries to validate virtual-screening enrichment; and pursuing the causal intervention experiments outlined above to confirm that physicochemical partitioning is not an optimization artefact. More broadly, we propose a community evaluation workflow for molecular MoE architectures with five components: (1) a parameter-matched dense ablation to isolate routing-specific predictive effects from raw capacity; (2) a frozen random-gate and fixed-partition null, with η² reported for both, to isolate routing-specific organizational effects from structure that may already be present in the backbone; (3) cross-dataset replication spanning high- and low-chemical-diversity libraries; (4) η² quantification of expert alignment against standard physicochemical descriptors, reported in full rather than selectively; and (5) transparent reporting of null results on classification and small-dataset regimes rather than selective reporting of favorable benchmarks alone.

---

## Conclusions

Sparse MoE routing provides a prediction-coupled partition of molecular representations whose expert assignments are associated with physicochemical descriptors, most consistently aromatic-ring count. MoE-GCN has a lower mean error than plain GCN on ten of twelve regression comparisons, but classification gains are inconsistent and the ablation does not establish an advantage over dense models of comparable or larger size. In controls that share the backbone, split and training protocol, fixed random assignment was worse than learned routing, whereas no consistent accuracy difference was detected between the learned router and an untrained random gate or a fixed descriptor partition. On Tox21 a trained gate (K ≥ 2) produced stronger descriptor association than K = 1, but a frozen random gate retained most of it at the same validation AUROC. Much of the physicochemical structure in expert assignments is therefore present without a trained gate, which is compatible with the backbone representation already encoding it (the backbone can adapt to a fixed gate, so this is an inference, not a direct test), and we detect no consistent accuracy benefit from training the router under the tested conditions. The contribution is a controlled evaluation procedure, with measured limits, for molecular MoE plug-ins; whether router training sharpens the partition needs more seeds, other datasets and other backbones.

## Methods

### Molecular representation

Each molecule is represented as an undirected graph in which nodes are atoms and edges are bonds. Atoms carry a nine-dimensional feature vector (atomic number, chirality, degree, formal charge, hydrogen count, radical electron count, hybridization, aromaticity and ring membership) and bonds a three-dimensional feature vector (bond type, stereochemistry and conjugation). Features were generated with RDKit [19] and cast to single precision at load time. The same nine-dimensional atom featurization was used for every model and dataset, with one exception: Caco-2 and AqSolDB in the dense-baseline ablation used six-dimensional atom features (see Dense-baseline ablation).

### Backbone architectures

GCN [4] is the primary backbone, on which all interpretability analyses and the dense-baseline ablation were performed: each layer applies the symmetric-normalized propagation **H**⁽ˡ⁺¹⁾ = σ(**D̂**⁻¹ᐟ²**Â** **D̂**⁻¹ᐟ²**H**⁽ˡ⁾**W**⁽ˡ⁾) with **Â** = **A** + **I**, followed by batch normalization, ReLU and dropout, with a global mean pooling read-out producing the molecular representation. As a secondary cross-architecture transferability test, we additionally used an edge-conditioned message-passing network (EC-MPNN) built from an NNConv operator with a GRUCell update rather than a reduction to standard convolution [6]; we inspected the implementation directly and confirmed it does not carry the reverse-bond message exclusion that defines Yang et al.'s original D-MPNN, so we do not claim fidelity to that architecture and refer to it as EC-MPNN throughout rather than D-MPNN or DMPNN. It was benchmarked against the plug-in on three MoleculeNet regression datasets. A graph isomorphism network (GIN) [5] with sum aggregation was implemented and is available in the accompanying repository, but was not included in any reported benchmark comparison or transferability test in this study; we make no claim about GIN transferability in either direction. All primary results and mechanistic conclusions in this study originate from the MoE-GCN configuration; the EC-MPNN results are presented only as a secondary robustness check. GCN, EC-MPNN, and GIN were all implemented in PyTorch Geometric.

### Sparse mixture-of-experts module

The MoE module is inserted between the pooled molecular representation **h** ∈ ℝᴰ and the task prediction head, leaving the backbone unchanged. A linear gating network scores the *E* experts, and only the top-*K* are activated:

**g**(**h**) = TopK(**W**g**h** + **b**g, *K*),  **W**g ∈ ℝ^{*E*×*D*}

w_i = softmax(g_i(**h**)) · 𝟙[i ∈ TopK],  Σ_i w_i = 1

y = Σ_{i ∈ TopK} w_i · FFNᵢ(**h**)

where each expert FFNᵢ is a single linear layer followed by ReLU, mapping ℝᴰ → ℝᴰ (`nn.Sequential(nn.Linear(D, D), nn.ReLU())` in the released code; earlier drafts called this a two-layer perceptron, which was incorrect). Non-selected logits are set to −∞ before the softmax so that exactly *K* experts contribute to each forward pass. To prevent the router from collapsing onto a single expert, we added a load-balancing auxiliary loss

L_bal = *E* · Σ_{i=1}^{*E*} ( mean_{x ∈ B} w_i(x) )²

This is the sum of squared mean *gate weights*, not the standard Switch-Transformer formulation [10], which multiplies the *dispatch fraction* (the hard fraction of tokens actually routed to each expert) by the *mean gate probability* for that expert. Our formulation is minimized at uniform gate weights and is therefore directionally correct, but it is computed on the post-top-*K* normalized weights, and for *K* = 1 those weights are identically one, so *L*_bal is constant and, like the task loss, sends no gradient to the router (the Tox21 trajectory in Fig. 2 and TDC Lipophilicity use *K* = 1). We optimized the total objective L = L_task + λ L_bal with λ = 0.01, where L_task is masked binary cross-entropy (classification, with missing labels excluded) or mean squared error (regression). The number of experts *E* ∈ {4, 8, 16} and the sparsity *K* ∈ [1, min(4, *E*)] were treated as hyperparameters.

Except for the dense-baseline ablation described below, all datasets were split by Bemis–Murcko scaffold [17]: molecules were first clustered by Bemis–Murcko core scaffold, and full scaffold clusters—not individual molecules—were then randomly assigned to train/validation/test in an 80/10/10 ratio, eliminating scaffold leakage across splits. A stratified scaffold split was used for the two smallest, most imbalanced classification sets (BBBP and BACE) to preserve class ratios across splits and avoid single-class validation folds. Hyperparameters were tuned per dataset with the Optuna tree-structured Parzen estimator [18] over 30 trials with median pruning. Models were trained with Adam and weight decay, a learning-rate schedule that halved the rate on validation plateaus, early stopping with patience 15, a maximum of 100 epochs and batch size 64. MoE-GCN is reported as the mean and standard deviation over five random seeds on MoleculeNet and three seeds on TDC. GCN is reported under the matching protocol for each benchmark: on MoleculeNet, GCN was retrained with an independent Optuna hyperparameter search of identical budget and search space to MoE-GCN (omitting only the expert-count and top-*K* parameters, which do not apply to a plain backbone) and evaluated over the same five scaffold-split seeds as MoE-GCN (see Table 1); for classification, GCN was rerun for BBBP, BACE, ClinTox, SIDER and Tox21 with the same 30-trial TPE search (seed 42) and search space (`run_gcn_matched_classif.py`), while ToxCast and HIV were not rerun and have no GCN value in Table 1; on TDC, GCN was likewise retrained with its own 30-trial Optuna search (TPE sampler seed 42, median pruner, hyper-parameter objective averaged over two seeds; search space identical to MoE-GCN except for the expert-count and top-*K* parameters) on the TDC default scaffold split (seed 42), with 100 maximum epochs and early stopping patience 15 for both models (`run_gcn_tdc_baseline.py`, `run_moegcn_tdc_benchmark.py`). GCN was evaluated over three seeds; MoE-GCN was run for five (seeds 0–4) and Table 2 and the pooled test use its first three (seeds 0–2) to match, and all 9 TDC regression win/loss directions are unchanged when all five seeds are used (`results_moegcn_tdc_v2.json`). Because MoE-GCN tunes two additional hyper-parameters within the same 30 trials, the search budget per parameter is, if anything, smaller for MoE-GCN (see Table 2). We use this matched protocol throughout, including in the pooled statistical test (see Statistical evaluation). All experiments ran on a single NVIDIA GTX 1660 Ti (6 GB).

### Dense-baseline ablation

To determine whether performance gains arise from the routing mechanism itself rather than from the additional parameters the expert bank introduces, we compared MoE-GCN against three non-routed baselines (parameter counts below), the first two designed to remove routing and the third to remove the expert bank altogether: Dense-uniform, in which the same expert subnetworks are retained but averaged with equal, non-learned weights (removing the routing signal while preserving expert capacity), Dense-wide, a single wider feed-forward layer (removing the multi-expert structure entirely), and Plain-GCN, the backbone with no expert module. Recorded parameter counts on ESOL, FreeSolv and Lipophilicity were 400,133 (MoE-GCN), 399,105 (Dense-uniform), 661,505 (Dense-wide) and 201,729 (Plain-GCN), so only Dense-uniform is parameter-matched; counts were not recorded in the result file for Caco-2 and AqSolDB. All four configurations (MoE-GCN and the three baselines) shared identical GCN backbones and training protocol and used one fixed set of hyper-parameters chosen in advance (hidden size 256, three GCN layers, dropout 0.1, learning rate 5 × 10⁻⁴, weight decay 10⁻⁵; for MoE-GCN four experts and K = 2); no hyper-parameter search was run in this ablation (`ablation_routing.py`, `ablation_routing_add_plain.py`). ESOL, FreeSolv and Lipophilicity used the Bemis–Murcko scaffold split. Caco-2 and AqSolDB used a random 80/10/10 split with the atom featurization of the original ablation script (six-dimensional: atomic number, degree, formal charge, hybridization, aromaticity and hydrogen count, as verified in `ablation_routing.py`), unlike the TDC scaffold split and nine-dimensional features used elsewhere in this study, so the five datasets are not on a single split protocol. In `ablation_routing.py` the split for each dataset is generated once, before the arm and seed loops, and reused for MoE-GCN, Dense-uniform and Dense-wide, so these three primary arms are paired on identical training, validation and test molecules. The scaffold split in this script is deterministic (largest scaffold groups assigned to training first), unlike the randomized scaffold-group assignment used elsewhere, and the random split is drawn from the global random-number state and was not saved; in both cases one split is used for all five seeds, so the seeds vary initialization and batch order but not the split, and the reported standard deviations exclude split variance. Plain-GCN was run by a separate script; whether Plain-GCN saw the same random split as the other three arms on Caco-2 and AqSolDB therefore cannot be confirmed, and we therefore restrict the Plain-GCN comparison to the three scaffold-split datasets. We evaluated all four on five regression datasets (ESOL, FreeSolv, Lipophilicity, Caco-2, AqSolDB-Solubility) over five seeds each. For each dataset, performance was averaged across the five seeds, yielding five dataset-level paired observations for each comparison; paired *t*-tests and Wilcoxon signed-rank tests were then applied to these dataset-level differences; with five datasets these tests are exploratory.

### Quantifying expert chemical specialization

To test whether routing is chemically meaningful, we extracted the dominant expert (the top-1 routed expert) for every molecule in a dataset by a forward pass with routing weights exposed, and related this categorical expert label to eight RDKit physicochemical descriptors: molecular weight, calculated LogP, hydrogen-bond acceptor and donor counts, topological polar surface area, rotatable-bond count, ring count and aromatic-ring count. For each descriptor we computed (i) the mutual information between expert label and the descriptor (discretized into bins, reported in Supplementary Table S1); (ii) a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) across expert groups; (iii) a non-parametric Kruskal–Wallis test; and (iv) the η² effect size, the fraction of descriptor variance explained by expert membership — (ii)–(iv) reported in Table 6. This analysis was performed independently on four datasets spanning distinct ADMET endpoints: aqueous solubility (AqSolDB, *n* = 9,980), Caco-2 permeability (Wang, *n* = 910), acute toxicity (LD50, Zhu, *n* = 7,385) and lipophilicity (AstraZeneca, *n* = 4,200). One of these four (AstraZeneca Lipophilicity) was selected as a putative low-diversity comparator based on the source literature; we measured chemical diversity directly with two standard metrics (Bemis–Murcko scaffold-to-molecule ratio and mean pairwise ECFP4 Tanimoto distance, Table S2), which disagree on whether this dataset is in fact the least diverse of the four. This design allows us to assess replication and to report, honestly, the limits of what our diversity measurements can explain about where specialization does and does not emerge. To assess partition reproducibility we computed the adjusted Rand index and normalized mutual information between the dominant-expert labels of every pair of the five seeds (10 pairs per dataset), with a permutation null (50 shuffles), from the saved routing files (`routing_*_seed*_v2.npy`).

As a second candidate mechanism, for the 21 TDC ADMET datasets with a non-collapsed router (PPBR-AZ excluded because routing collapsed to a single expert), we computed the absolute Pearson correlation between the prediction endpoint and each of the eight descriptors, and correlated it with that descriptor's η² (mean over five seeds) using Spearman's rank correlation across all dataset–descriptor pairs (Table S3). Uncertainty was assessed with a bootstrap that resamples datasets (2,000 resamples), since pairs are nested within datasets. This tests whether descriptors driving the endpoint are suppressed as specialization axes, as would be expected if they became task-relevant signal rather than a nuisance axis available for the router to partition on.

### Specialization-versus-gain correlation

Across the 21 TDC ADMET datasets with a non-collapsed router, we correlated each dataset's largest single-descriptor η² (mean over five seeds) with MoE-GCN's percentage performance gain over plain GCN (RMSE or MAE reduction for regression datasets, AUROC or Spearman increase otherwise, as tabulated in Table 2) using Spearman's rank correlation, with a bootstrap over datasets (5,000 resamples) for the interval. This tests whether stronger specialization is associated with larger predictive benefit.

### Routing controls and forced-swap test

For the four regression datasets of the specialization analysis, we trained four arms with the released backbone, expert modules, hyper-parameters (`BEST_PARAMS`), TDC scaffold split (seed 42), optimizer and early-stopping protocol of the main experiments, differing in how experts are selected (the fixed k-means and fixed random arms also use hard top-1 routing, whereas the learned and frozen arms use the dataset's tuned K). *Learned*: the paper's router. *Frozen gate*: same, with gate parameters excluded from the optimizer. *Fixed k-means*: hard top-1 assignment from k-means (E clusters, `n_init=10`) on eight standardized RDKit descriptors, fitted on training molecules only and applied to validation and test molecules. *Fixed random*: one expert index drawn uniformly at random for each molecule from a seeded generator (`numpy.random.default_rng(1000 + seed)`), drawn once and stored as a label on every training, validation and test molecule, so the assignment is identical across epochs and evaluation calls; it uses hard top-1 routing and is independent of molecular structure (it is not a hash or function of the molecule), so it carries no information about unseen molecules and tests whether a partition that ignores chemistry performs as well. Seeds 0–4 were used (0–2 for LD50). Test MAE is the metric; arms were compared by paired differences over seeds with one-sample *t*-tests, uncorrected for multiplicity and with low power. Forced swap: on each trained learned or frozen-gate model, every test molecule was forced through each expert *j*; M[g][j] is the mean absolute error of molecules whose natural dominant expert is *g* when forced to expert *j*; the reported ratio is the mean over active groups *g* of the mean over *j* ≠ *g* of M[g][j] divided by the mean of M[g][g], with active groups defined as those with ≥ 10 test molecules. Forced routing replaces the natural gate weights with a one-hot weight on expert *j*, so both the own-expert and the other-expert errors are single-expert predictions; they differ from the natural prediction, which for K ≥ 2 combines K experts (natural test MAE is recorded separately). The ratio therefore measures expert-specific error, not the effect of replacing a molecule's natural top-K route. Gate gradients were checked with a dummy loss plus the balance loss on the repository's `MoELayer`. The step 0 check and arms are in `exp1_routing_controls.py`; seed-wise η² is in `exp0_eta2_across_seeds.py`.

### Router-training controls on Tox21 and Lipophilicity

We trained MoE-GCN on Tox21 (MoleculeNet, 12 tasks, E = 4, hidden 256, 4 layers; scaffold split in the `training_curves.py` order) and on TDC AstraZeneca Lipophilicity (`BEST_PARAMS`) with K ∈ {1, 2, 4}, seeds 0–2 (K = 2 on Tox21 additionally seeds 3–4, learned and frozen), for a fixed 80 epochs with no early stopping, selecting the best validation epoch. Per epoch we recorded the fraction of training molecules whose dominant expert is each expert (eval mode), the gate-weight norm, mean routing entropy, training loss and validation score (AUROC averaged over tasks for Tox21; MAE for Lipophilicity). At the best epoch we extracted dominant-expert labels for all molecules and computed η² for the eight descriptors (experts with ≥ 10 molecules). The frozen-gate control (K = 2, Tox21) excluded the gate parameters from the optimizer. Paired differences (learned minus frozen, same seed) used one-sample *t*-tests over seeds with *t*-based 95% intervals; the η² comparisons across eight descriptors are exploratory and uncorrected. Code: `exp2_topk_dynamics.py`.

### Statistical evaluation

All significance testing in this study follows a single consolidated protocol, reported here rather than scattered across Results. The primary statistical unit for the pooled regression test is the dataset (*n* = 12: 3 MoleculeNet + 9 TDC), not the individual seed: for each dataset we used the mean MoE-GCN value across seeds and the corresponding GCN value as reported in its source table — a three-seed mean for the nine TDC datasets, and a five-seed mean for the three MoleculeNet regression datasets (matched protocol, Table 1) — giving one paired observation per dataset. Lipophilicity is included in both suites and therefore contributes two non-independent comparisons; a sensitivity analysis counting it once is reported in Table 4. Because TDC regression datasets mix metrics with opposite improvement directions (MAE, ↓ better; Spearman correlation, ↑ better), we defined a signed improvement score per dataset — (GCN − MoE-GCN) for MAE-scored datasets and (MoE-GCN − GCN) for Spearman-scored datasets — so that a positive value always indicates MoE-GCN improved over GCN. Because these signed scores mix RMSE, MAE, and Spearman differences on incommensurate numerical scales, we do not treat the magnitude of a signed-rank test on the raw values as fully reliable, and instead take an exact one-sided sign (binomial) test on win/loss counts as the primary pooled test, since it depends only on which model won each dataset and not on the relative size of wins measured in different units. We report the Wilcoxon signed-rank test on the same signed scores as a sensitivity analysis, one-sided (alternative: MoE > baseline) with the rank-biserial correlation as effect size. For the dense-baseline ablation, the five datasets share the RMSE metric but not its scale, so we test relative RMSE differences, (other − MoE-GCN) / MoE-GCN, at the dataset level (*n* = 5; one value per dataset, the mean over five seeds) with a paired *t*-test and a Wilcoxon signed-rank test, both two-sided; with five datasets the smallest attainable two-sided Wilcoxon *P* is 0.0625 and the *t*-test's normality assumption cannot be checked, so these tests are descriptive and the confidence intervals are the primary result. Effect sizes are reported where relevant: the rank-biserial correlation for the pooled Wilcoxon test, η² for descriptor specialization, and paired differences with confidence intervals for the control comparisons. Wording implying significance is used only where *P* ≤ 0.05.
